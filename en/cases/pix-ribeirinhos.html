Pix for everyone: field research with a riverside community in the Amazon

Field research with 12 residents of a riverside community in Pará, Brazil. Most did not recognize the Pix symbol, and the study produced 14 design recommendations.

My role

Analysis of the data collected in the field (interviews, icon interpretation and prototype interaction).

Type
Field research with underrepresented users, in financial inclusion
Methods
Semi-structured interviews, icon analysis, prototype testing and the Communicability Evaluation Method (CEM)
Participants
12 residents of a riverside community in Pará, aged 26 to 74
Team
Luciano Arruda Teran, Alan Silva, Caio Carvalho, Sandy Farias, Suzane Santos, Fabiola Araujo, Cleidson de Souza and Marcelle Mota (UFPA, USP and UFPR)
Published outcome
IHC 2023, ACM

Summary

Pix, Brazil's instant payment system, was created to promote financial inclusion, but its apps are designed for people who already master smartphones and have good internet. We went to a riverside community in the Amazon to understand how people deal with digital payments and to test a Pix prototype designed for them. Most people did not recognize the Pix symbol itself, and the study produced 14 design recommendations for instant payment apps.

Why this project matters to me

My grandparents are from a riverside community. I know firsthand the distance between what a banking app expects from its user and the reality of people who live by the river, with limited internet and little access to formal schooling. This helped me read the data carefully and without judgment.

The problem

People with little schooling who live far from urban centers and have limited internet are called "emergent users" in the literature. They increasingly depend on digital services to receive pensions, retirement benefits and government aid. But payment interfaces use icons, terms and flows that assume prior experience.

Research question

What communication barriers do riverside residents face in instant payment apps, and what do they need to use Pix independently?

Process

  1. Inspection of a banking app and of the Central Bank rules for Pix, to identify accessibility requirements.
  2. Prototype of an instant payment flow based on guidelines for emergent users: short forms, instructions of up to 50 characters and examples in the fields.
  3. Field research in the community, with individual sessions in three stages:
    • Interview about income, access to information, phone use and relationship with the bank.
    • Paper-based icon analysis: people said what each symbol meant to them.
    • Prototype use in four tasks: first access, creating a Pix key, paying and receiving.
  4. Analysis of voice and screen recordings with the Communicability Evaluation Method, which classifies each moment of confusion ("Where is it?", "What now?", "Oops!", "What's this?").
  5. Design recommendations based on the evidence, cross-checked with the literature.

Ethics: approved by the Ethics Committee, with participants' consent and anonymized data (P1 to P12).

Who took part

  • 9 women and 3 men, aged 26 to 74.
  • Income from açaí harvesting, fishing, retirement, pensions, nursing, cooking and private tutoring.
  • Varied schooling: from incomplete elementary school to a college degree.
  • Internet access by phone, limited by data credits.
  • Almost all received some government aid, such as Bolsa Família.

Key findings

1. App icons did not speak people's language

  • 9 of the 12 participants did not understand the Pix symbol.
  • The money icon was read as a "camera", "photo camera" or "playing card", and four people could not say what it was.
  • Everyone understood the key icon, but with different meanings, such as "Pix key" or "security key".

2. Age shaped the relationship with the bank

People aged 26 to 41 checked their balance in the app. People aged 50 to 74 learned about payments from the previous receipt, asked relatives or went to the branch, and reported difficulties with apps.

3. Family is part of the journey

Several people relied on children or relatives to make transfers. Help from someone they trust is a real means of access, not a detail.

4. There were 61 communication breakdowns

Breakdowns concentrated in four points: first access, Pix key creation, payment and receiving. The most frequent were "What now?", "Where is it?" and "Oops!".

5. Order and examples reduced anxiety

Participants said the sequence of steps made them calmer when making a payment. Examples in the fields and support text helped them fill in information correctly.

Design recommendations (14 in total, 6 highlighted)

Recommendation Evidence
Accept email, CPF (taxpayer ID) or phone in a single field, detecting the type automatically People typed directly, without choosing the key type
Use illustrations close to real objects and to people's context Abstract icons were not recognized
Split the payment into simple questions: "How much?", "To whom?", "What for?" Long forms and scrolling were confusing
Allow going back and fixing one step without redoing everything Previous frustrating experiences with banking apps
Show the keys already created when receiving money People had to retype the key they had just created
Offer audio and animated guidance Little familiarity with text and with apps

Limitations

  • A single case study, in one community, with 12 participants.
  • The prototype did not have all the functions of a real banking app.

What I learned

Icons that seem universal are not. The Pix symbol, known across the country, meant nothing to most people in the community. Research with people who live far from urban centers requires setting aside what the team considers "obvious" and listening to how each person actually reads the interface.
