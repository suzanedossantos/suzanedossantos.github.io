EmoFrame: bringing emotional assessment instruments from paper to digital

Participatory design with health professionals and four forms of evaluation. High SUS scores, and concrete problems revealed by interviews and heuristic evaluation.

My role

Lead researcher on the project, from the literature mapping to planning and analyzing the evaluations. I also moderated the usability test sessions.

Type
Research and design of a tool, from discovery to evaluation
Context
Master's in Computer Science, ICMC-USP (2022), advised by Prof. Kamila Rios da Hora Rodrigues
Methods
Systematic mapping, participatory design, evolutionary prototyping, usability testing, interviews, heuristic evaluation and evaluation with domain experts
Participants
11 in the usability test and 3 HCI experts in the heuristic evaluation, plus Psychology and Gerontology experts
Partners
Psychology, Gerontology and Computing professionals
Published outcome
Master's dissertation (ICMC-USP) and papers at HCII 2022

Summary

Computing professionals often build systems for other fields, such as health, but the partnership with experts usually ends before real use. As a result, developers get no feedback on how people feel when using the solution. EmoFrame brings together, in digital form, instruments for assessing emotional responses and guides the choice of the most suitable one.

I combined four forms of evaluation. The questionnaires gave high scores, and it was the interviews and the heuristic evaluation that showed where the experience failed.

The problem

  • Assessing users' emotional responses is hard for computing professionals, who rarely have training for it.
  • Many validated instruments exist only on paper, which makes it easy to lose responses and slows down analysis.
  • The lack of feedback after delivery prevents improving the solution based on real experience.

Research question

Does a tool that brings together digital emotional assessment instruments help professionals evaluate their systems more easily, without losing the quality of the original instruments?

Process

1. Understanding what already exists

I carried out a systematic mapping of instruments for assessing emotional responses. It guided the choice of instruments in the framework and was published at HCII 2022.

2. Building with the people who use it

I used participatory design with evolutionary prototyping. Psychology and Gerontology professionals took part in workshops and reviewed the prototypes several times until each instrument was validated. In the end, the framework brought together seven instruments, such as SAM, PANAS and SUS.

3. Evaluating in four complementary ways

Evaluation With whom What it looked for
Domain experts A psychologist and gerontologists, including the creator of one of the instruments Fidelity to the original instrument
Usability testing 11 people from Design, HCI and Psychology, in in-person sessions with 11 tasks, SUS and an interview Ease of use and perceived value
Heuristic evaluation 3 HCI experts, with Nielsen's 10 heuristics, personas and a severity scale Interface problems, by priority
Consolidation Meeting with the 3 evaluators Consensus on the problems and the severity of each

Key findings

1. The scores were high, but they did not tell everything

All 11 participants gave SUS scores above 80, between 86 and 100. Even so, the interviews and the heuristic evaluation found concrete problems.

2. Perceived value was in the automatic analysis

The most cited benefit was not having to calculate results by hand.

"The main benefit is how easy it is to apply the questionnaires, especially for the analysis phase, because the system already delivers the calculated results." (P3)

"I think EmoFrame can democratize access to evaluation tools, as well as automate the analysis of results." (P5)

3. The system lacked feedback

Missing or unclear error messages appeared in both the test and the heuristic evaluation.

"When I made a mistake in the email, it didn't say what the error was, and when I searched for the name in 'Results' there was no warning like 'Not found'." (P9)

4. Information about the instruments was missing

The heuristic evaluation started with 24 problems, which dropped to 20 after consolidation. The three most severe (severity 4) were in error prevention, flexibility of use and help and documentation. For the evaluators, the lack of information at the start and in each instrument was what most hurt the experience. This confirmed what the usability test had already suggested.

5. Results could only be viewed one at a time

"The main difficulty would be compiling the research data, because it shows complete information, but only individually, which makes comparison difficult." (P3)

6. For the experts, digital solves problems of paper

"On paper, there may be 'missings' [...]. Besides, it is almost impossible to shuffle the items in each print. These two things don't happen in digital." (Psychology expert)

The creator of the Gerontological Care Plan (PAGe), an instrument with 104 questions, helped adjust the digital version. She asked, for example, to separate individual and family income, to open a free-text field when the person selects "Other" and to keep the administration instructions in each question.

What changed as a result

Finding Decision
Severity 4 problems Fix first, following the order of severity
Missing information about the instruments Add a tutorial, full name, version and documentation for each instrument
Missing error messages Give clear feedback at login, in searches and in required fields
Individual-only results Plan a comparative view of the data
Adjustments requested by experts Incorporated into the digital instruments, including PAGe

EmoFrame became the foundation of my PhD, where the framework was expanded to support the entire evaluation planning process.

Limitations

  • Usability test participants were from the research group itself and knew the field well, which probably contributed to the high SUS scores.
  • The test took place during the pandemic, with health protocols and in-person sessions in a controlled environment.

What I learned

A high SUS score does not mean there are no problems. Combining a questionnaire, interviews and heuristic evaluation showed what each method missed, and when the methods agreed, it became clearer what to fix first. I also learned that, in a project with other fields, validation by domain experts is as important as usability: a digital instrument that is easy to use but unfaithful to the original is of no use to anyone.
