ePAGe: who can interpret the results of an older adult health instrument?

Digitization of a Gerontology instrument and a study comparing how experts and non-experts read its charts and propose referrals.

My role

First author and lead researcher. I planned the study, prepared and anonymized the materials, recruited the participants and analyzed the responses.

Type
Qualitative research on understanding data and visualizations
Methods
Participatory design and evolutionary prototyping (ePAGe), followed by a qualitative study comparing how experts and non-experts interpret results
Participants
4 professionals (2 gerontologists and 2 Computing/HCI professionals), analyzing 7 real anonymized results
Team
Suzane Santos, Brunela Orlandi, Marcelle Mota and Kamila Rodrigues (USP and UFPA)
Published outcome
IHC 2023, ACM

Summary

PAGe is a Gerontology instrument with 104 questions that assesses the vulnerability of older adults. First, the instrument was digitized in partnership with gerontologists. Then a practical question arose: without a gerontologist on the team, could other people understand the charts and act on them? The results showed that both profiles understood the visualizations, but domain experience made a difference in the quality of the referrals.

Context

PAGe is part of the protocol of a digital literacy course for older adults (60+) offered by USP since 2015, within its Open University for Older Adults. The instrument helps the team get to know each student and suggest referrals.

Until 2021, a gerontologist on the team applied PAGe and interpreted the results. From 2022 on, this was no longer possible, and the end-of-course assessment stopped happening.

Stage 1: taking the instrument off paper

The paper version of PAGe is long and requires calculations across domains. The digital version (ePAGe) was built with participatory design and evolutionary prototyping:

  • Requirements gathering with a partner gerontologist.
  • Medium- and high-fidelity prototypes reviewed by her in cycles, until validation.
  • Evaluation of the working version with the gerontologist and with the creator of the original instrument.

Outcome: calculations became automatic, and the system instantly generates a score table and two radar charts (demand map and domain map).

Stage 2: testing whether the results are understandable

Research question

Can a person without Gerontology training look at PAGe results and propose referrals similar to those of an expert?

Process

  1. I anonymized 7 real PAGe results, applied in 2022, removing personal data and dates through code.
  2. I defined the scope: participants saw only the visual elements (score table, risk classification and the two radar charts).
  3. I recruited 4 participants: 2 Gerontology experts (E1 and E2) and 2 non-experts from Computing with HCI training (NE1 and NE2).
  4. Asynchronous, independent study: each participant received the materials by email, without seeing the others' answers, and filled in three fields for each result: main observations, suggested referrals and other comments.
  5. I compared the analyses across and within profiles.

Ethics: approved by the USP Ethics Committee, with the older adults' identities protected at every stage.

Key findings

1. The charts worked for both profiles

All four participants identified the highest-risk demands from the charts and generally agreed on where action was needed. In the seventh result, for example, all of them pointed to malnutrition and suggested follow-up with a nutritionist.

2. Experience with the instrument mattered more than the degree

The difference was not only between experts and non-experts, but also within each group:

  • E1, who already applied PAGe, related demands to one another, separated immediate, medium- and long-term interventions, and in some cases disagreed with the automatic risk classification based on what she saw in the scores.
  • E2, a gerontologist unfamiliar with PAGe, made more generic suggestions, following general protocols of the field.
  • NE2, who had used ePAGe before, made readings closer to the experts' than NE1, who did not know the instrument.

3. Domain knowledge shows in the referrals

The non-experts identified the problems, but the experts proposed more complete referrals, considering several aspects of the older adult's life.

What changed as a result

Finding Implication
The charts let non-experts identify risks PAGe can stay in the course protocol, even without a gerontologist present
Familiarity with the instrument improves analysis Train those who apply it and keep reading guidance next to the results
Complete referrals depend on experts Use non-expert analysis as screening, not as a replacement for the professional

Limitations

  • Proof of concept with 4 participants recruited by convenience.
  • The non-experts had HCI training, which may have made the charts easier to read.

What I learned

A visualization is understood when people grasp what it means and can act on it, not just when they can read it. Comparing different profiles looking at the same data showed what the charts communicated on their own and what depended on each person's background. This kind of study applies to any dashboard whose readers are not experts in the subject.
